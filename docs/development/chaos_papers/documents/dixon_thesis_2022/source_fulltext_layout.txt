Abstract
In this thesis we quantify the generation of higher order tones in Josephson Travelling Wave Parametric
Amplifiers (JTWPAs). We discuss the background theory of parametric amplification through optical
non-linear wave mixing. Following this we show a set of experiments in which we observe unexpected
tones. The first experiment describes a resonant device and gives an overview of standard measurement
procedures and an insight into Josephson device behaviour. In the resonant device we demonstrate a
single higher order tone being generated at a frequency which can be mapped back to the three wave
mixing condition. The second experiment involves a more complex travelling wave device. Here we
show that with a single tone one can generate a cascade of higher order harmonics. We then show that
in the case of two input tones one can detect responses at various combinations of sums and differences
of the two tones. This section motivates the rest of the work done in this thesis.
In order to gain insight into our experimental observations we use a circuit simulation software
which includes the Josephson junction circuit element. We confirm the ability to model linear transmis-
sion lines with the software. We then explore the Josephson junction model and produce the standard
results. We then model the travelling wave design which consists of a standard transmission line
embedded with rf SQUIDs. Finally we discuss the observation of higher order tones in the simulation
which are not present in the theory and relate the frequencies of these tones back to the tones observed
in our experiment.
With a focus on these higher order tones we extend the standard coupled mode theory to allow for
these higher order tones to be considered. We also consider the approximations made in the theory and
subsequently alter the simulation to ensure their validity. We then finally resolve any discrepancies
between the simulation and theory. This work forms the principles of my first manuscript which in turn
has led to the further development of ideas to combat such higher order tones

1.1
Motivation
Quantum computing was first envisaged by Richard Feynman in 1982. The proposal was simple, if
a system can be reduced such that quantum states can be differentiated between and manipulated,
then quantum mechanical behaviour can be efficiently simulated. This began the race for a quantum
computer, whose processor would be made of quantum bits (qubits). These are typically two level
systems (TLS), where the considered states of the system are the two lowest energy level states. These
qubits can then be in either of the energy states or any linear superposition of the states. Combining
this with entanglement between qubits would allow for a quantum processor to rapidly outperform a
classical processor comprising of classical bits for certain problems.
Over the last 40 years, a lot of focus was devoted to the development of the qubit. Qubits came
in various forms such as; Superconducting microwave qubits, optical ion trap qubits, semiconductor
quantum dots, and diamond nitrogen vacancy (NV) centres. Each of these have attached pros and cons.
However, at the time of writing this thesis, the first work claiming to demonstrate quantum supremacy
was achieved using superconducting microwave qubits [1].
This “flavour” of superconducting microwave qubits has risen to prominence and is now widely
regarded as the front running technology for a realisable practical quantum computer. From the first
demonstration of superconducting qubits [2] the community has rapidly evolved to overcome many of
the engineering challenges of making, manipulating and measuring these qubits. For the purpose of
this thesis we will solely discuss this superconducting microwave technology and further mention of

Fundamentally, any useful qubit will always suffer from a near-paradoxical point. In order
to maintain quantum mechanical behaviour, the qubit must be isolated from the highly classical
environment. However to be useful, we as classical entities, must interact with it. This has led to further
development in the wider community. In the same way that a classical computer is more than just a
processor, a practical quantum computer is more than just a qubit. So following this, focus shifted
to further hardware considerations for use in quantum information processing. Of these, low-noise
amplifiers have been quintessential in the reliable measurement of qubits.
Typically superconducting qubits emit photons at the transition energy corresponding to the
difference between the ground and first excited state. For the superconducting qubits these photons are
in the microwave regime f ∼5 GHz and thus have energies of order Ef ≈3.3 × 10−24 J. To aid in
the detection of these the “Josephson Parametric Amplifier” (JPA) has been developed whose noise is
limited by the standard quantum limit [3]. The underlying physics of parametric amplification via the
non-linear response of the Josephson junction is the key phenomena which is studied in this thesis. The
Josephson Travelling Wave Parametric Amplifier (JTWPA) is a broadband extension of the JPA which
at the time of writing this thesis is the state of the art technology in broadband, low noise amplification
and is the main focus of this thesis.

1.3.1
Josephson Parametric Amplifiers
The first mention of JPA measurements was made by H. Zimmer in 1967 [15]. By depositing a thin
strip diagonally across a face on a cube rutile resonator, oxidising in atmosphere for 15 minutes and
then depositing another thin strip at right angles they formed the Josephson junction. This was not
a well controlled procedure and by Zimmer’s own admission “... the formation of a junction in an
evaporated layer is more or less a question a lucky chance...”.
Critically, the junction was known to be a dissipation-less circuit element below its critical current.
With this the attention turned to noise in Josephson circuits. It was known that, by the Heisenberg
uncertainty principle, any bosonic signal must incorporate an uncertainty (S(ω)) of at least S(ω) =
ℏω/2. In 1982, Caves [3], showed that a “phase-insensitive” amplifier must add at least this noise due
to quantum mechanics. However, Caves also showed that this is overcome if one is only interested in
one quadrature phase of the signal. In this case noise can be “squeezed” in the measured quadrature
at the expense of added noise in the unmeasured quadrature. Kuzmin, Likharev, Migulin and Zorin
studied these noise properties directly in the case of JPAs [16]. They found that by operating a JPA in
the degenerate mode, that is where the signal and idler frequency are the same fs = fi, then the added
noise can indeed be lower than the standard quantum limit.
Three years later, in 1985, Smith et. al. [17] specifically measured a JPA in terms of its noise
properties. While a low noise temperature TN ≈6 K was deduced, they also highlighted many of the
sub-optimal properties of the device, which are closely related to this thesis. Firstly, they measured the
device in the three wave mixing (3WM) condition, (fp = fs + fi). Second, they noted the possibility
of other idler frequencies, predominantly the pump harmonic f2p = 2fp or near zero frequency terms,
analysis of these further generated tones form a substantial discussion in this thesis.
Following this, engineering steps were made to optimise the device and in 1989 Yurke measured
both the gain as well as the noise squeezing properties of the JPA [18]. While this demonstration of an
amplifier operating at the fundamental limit of quantum mechanics was novel, practical requirements
for such an amplifier were sparse.
A decade later in 1999 Nakamura, Pashkin and Tsai demonstrated the capability to control the
quantum mechanical states of a two level system (TLS) solid-state device, named the cooper-pair
28
Chapter 1. Introduction
box [2]. This discovery was the first time macroscopic control over the quantum states was shown in
solid-state superconducting devices.
Following this discovery, Wallraff et. al. [19] demonstrated that by embedding such a TLS in
a resonator one can make a continuous transmission measurement by measuring the transmission
properties of the resonator to infer the qubit state. This work led the way for coupling between the
photonic behaviour of qubits to the electrical properties of microwave resonators. Due to the delicate
nature of the measurement previous work in JPAs was revisited. Since JPAs already operate in the
microwave regime, and had been shown to introduce low noise to the system, the community quickly
took to re-purposing these devices in the context of quantum information processing readout tools.
At the same time other single-shot dispersive readout systems were being considered. For example,
Siddiqi et. al. demonstrated a Josephson Bifurcation Amplifier (JBA) [20] which was then used for
single shot qubit readout [21]. Biasing the JJ near a bifurcation point of the system means that an
additional, small, rf-drive can tip the system into one of two distinct oscillation states. These meta-stable
states then apply low back action. Also, by the nature of a bifurcation, these two states are distinct and
stable meaning a latched readout scheme can be achieved. JBAs have been used in qubit readout as
already mentioned as well as platforms for so-called quantum non-demolition measurement [22]. The
latching bifurcation style readout has also been extended to more exotic HyQUID type devices [23] and
Josephson Parametric Oscillators (JPOs) [24,25]. The latter of these also reported single-shot qubit
readout [26] with a fidelity (maximum state discrimination) of 81.5%.
While many of these system demonstrated promising results for single shot readout of a qubit
the community was aware of the scalability of such schemes. The resonant nature of all of these
devices constrained the bandwidth of operation to be within the line-width of the cavity modes. By
utilising two junction Superconducting QUantum Interference Devices (DC SQUIDs) Castellanos-
Beltran and Lehnart [27] were able to tune the resonant mode of a superconducting cavity between
4 −7.8 GHz by applying magnetic flux to adjust the DC SQUID inductance. In this experiment the
JPA was measured over nearly an octave (#octave = log2(fmax/fmin)) of frequencies by adjusting
the magnetic field. They also achieved up to 28 dB gain. The noise temperature TN in the degenerate
case fp = fs = fi ≈6.95 GHz was found to be TN ≈300 mK where the standard quantum limit
noise temperature at this frequency would be ℏω/2kB ≈166 mK, inferring that the input noise of the
amplifier was less than twice the standard quantum limit.

1.3.2
Josephson Travelling Wave Parametric Amplifiers
Discussions around general travelling wave parametric amplifiers predate the Josephson junction [30].
These devices were called upon for broadband sensitive amplification and again relied upon a non-
linear component to activate the parametric interaction and the wave mixing. These devices were then
reviewed from the point of view of superconducting solid state devices.
Initially, the kinetic inductance of the superconducting film itself provided the non-linearity in so
called Kinetic Inductance Travelling Wave Parametric Amplifiers (KITWPA) [31]. Here, the critical
current of a thin superconducting wire (I∗) gives an upper bound and obvious current scale, much
like the critical current of a JJ (Ic). Well below this critical current one can realise a simple, non-
linear, current dependent result for the kinetic inductance, Lk ≈Lk(0)[1 + (I/I∗)2] [31]. Typically
Lk << LT where LT is the total inductance of the line. This means that the non-linearity is very
weak in the superconducting wire. However using Titanium nitride (TiN) or Niobium-Titanium nitride
(NbTiN) one can increase the normal state resistivity by up to three orders of magnitude [32] which
drastically increases the relative non-linearity of the system.
Such a device was developed and tested by Eom et. al [31]. It was known that harmonic generation
of the pump tone will be the largest generated tone in a bare device. As such, band gap engineering
was utilised by implementing periodic loading along the line. This blocks the generation of the third
harmonic of the pump, and the subsequent cascade of tone generation via waves mixing with this tone.
The coupled mode theory for kinetic inductance devices is very similar to that of Josephson based
30
Chapter 1. Introduction
devices, which are the focus of this thesis.
This coupled mode theory was then extended to include JJs embedded in a transmission line by
Yaakobi et. al. [33]. Here the JJs replace the linear inductor in a standard ladder type transmission
line to form a Josephson Travelling Wave Parametric Amplifier (JTWPA). With this one can consider
the finite differences between voltage to ground at successive nodes and convert this to flux through
Maxwells equations. In the limit that the unit cell size between nodes is sufficiently smaller than the
wavelength of waves considered these finite differences are converted to a differential wave equation.
These coupled mode equations are discussed in detail in appendix A.2.6.
The theory for JTWPA was then further extended to include resonant phase matching by O’Brien
et. al. [34]. They describe a process whereby simultaneously diminishing the phase matching of
up-converted tones (SFG, harmonic generation) and promoting the phase matching of down-converted
tones (DFG, signal amplification) one can achieve larger and more broadband gain [34]. This is
achieved by capacitively coupling resonators along the line. One can then tune the dispersion relation
of the device such that an asymptotic pole is produced at the resonators frequency. If the pump is then
situated near this frequency, the pump phase can be adjusted, in a tight frequency range, anywhere
between ±π/2. This allows for a linear relationship between pump, signal and idler wave vectors. This
resonant phase matching can also cause a greater phase mismatch in higher frequency tones, reducing
the up-conversion to higher modes.
This idea was developed and tested by T. C. White et. al. [35] in 2015. The device consisted of 1326
JJs in total, separated into 17 sections of 78 JJs each. Each section had a λ/4 resonator capacitively
coupled at its centre. The spacing between resonators was also carefully chosen to be at a spacing
sufficient to suppress third harmonic generation of the pump, as per the band gap engineering in the
KITWPA mentioned previously. However this device only achieved 6 −8 dB of gain. This was then
increased by joining two devices together, however the target 20 dB was not shown.
Shortly after this C. Macklin. et. al. also demonstrated a resonant phase matched JTWPA [36].
This time it consisted of 2037 JJs and 700 resonators spaced every three JJs. This led to considerable
improvements in the gain and bandwidth characteristics, G > 20 dB over a 3 GHz bandwidth. This
coupled with the large bandwidth, high gain and low pump power requirements defined the state-of-the-
art in JTWPA experiments at the start of this project. The main problems with this device were gain
ripples associated with improper impedance matching. Also a pump power dependent phase shift can

that tuning the flux in a rf SQUID can alter the symmetry of the system. Biasing the rf SQUID with
flux, thus producing a circulating current, initiates a phase difference over the junctions. Since the rf
SQUIDs are all symmetric and oriented identically the phase drop across the junctions caused by the
screening current is identical. When this phase drop φ is 0 or a multiple of π then no current is flowing
through the junction. This can be seen from the Josephson relation eq. (1.25). In this case there is
no directionality to the biased system. However at any other flux bias a net current flows through the
junction and the system inherits a directionality based on the sign of this current. At flux bias positions
Φ = (2n + 1)Φ0/2 =⇒φ = nπ/2 the current flowing through the JJ is biased at its critical current

Zorin shows that by expanding the rf SQUID current phase relation about this even point one can
operate in the “three wave mixing” (3WM) scheme. This has certain benefits over the 4WM scheme
and is discussed in detail in this thesis.
The fabrication of such a device is simpler than the RPM JTWPAs as it doesn’t require additional
insulator layers. The footprint of the device is also reduced without the need for additional resonators.

Control of the Centrosymmetry
A key feature of the rf SQUID JTWPA is the ability to control the strength of β and γ with an external
flux. We calculate these through the transcendental equation eq. (1.29) and plot as per Fig. 2 of [14],
which we have reproduced in fig. 2.3, for a range of SQUID parameters (βL).
Coupling magnetic flux into the rf SQUID loop produces a non-zero phase drop across the junction.
When φDC ̸= 0, sin(φDC) ̸= 0 and thus the even terms in the φAC expansion contribute. This means
the current phase relationship is non-symmetric about its centre, mathematically I(−φAC) ̸= −I(φAC),
or non-centrosymmetric.

inherently brings γ = 0 (blue) and thus by tuning the DC phase (via tuning the flux threading the
SQUID loop) we tune the contribution of even and odd terms in the expansion.

wave mixing (3WM)” (non-centrosymmetric) while the γ = maximum, β = 0 coefficient describes
a “four wave mixing (4WM)” mechanism (centrosymmetric). In an unbiased scheme the parametric

Summary
Here, we have discussed in detail the measurement of a resonant device. The simplicity of measuring
within the line width of resonant modes removed much of the frequency dependence from the mea-
surement. Yet there are still complications in describing the mixing processes observed within the
device. These observations led to a consideration of the effects in potential up-conversion to higher
tones in a three wave mixing scheme. We note that off resonance pumping has been demonstrated in
similar devices [48] using three wave mixing however higher order tone generation was not studied.
This higher order tone generation has the potential to hamper device performance in a travelling wave
device, which is free from resonant constraints and allows propagation of tones over a continuous
bandwidth. As such, we now move to experiments with two travelling wave devices where we explore
and demonstrate the generation of these higher order tones.
3.2
Higher Order Tones in Travelling Wave Device
Following from the observation of potential up conversion in resonant devices, we also measure two
travelling wave devices sent from partners at Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt (PTB) named
JTWPA1 and JTWPA2. These devices consist of N ≈1000 rf SQUIDs embedded in a co-planar
wave-guide transmission line. The devices are first and second generation travelling wave parametric
amplifiers respectively. For this thesis, we focus on the observation of higher order tone generation and
how this can hamper parametric amplification. The most dominant tone generation is suspected to be
pump harmonics. As such, we first discuss the case of a single input tone, the pump tone, and how that
generates harmonics. We then look at two input tones, pump and signal, and how a myriad of tones are
generated from these.

Summary
We have made a number of experimental observations that show the existence of tones which are
not considered in the standard treatment of parametric amplifiers. First, we showed in a resonant
based device that we could explain the existence of a tone situated at the third mode of the resonator

experimental set up that these tones may be generated in travelling wave parametric amplifiers. It is
these observations that motivate the rest of the thesis, where we analyse the generation of higher order
tones in more detail.

We have shown in this section that WRspice is able to accurately model simple transmission lines
through a lumped element analysis. Furthermore, we have touched upon other practical considerations
such as frequency dependent insertion loss and impedance mismatch which are not routinely discussed
in the current literature. We have then shown how this is modified with the inclusion of a linear
shunt capacitor to increase the dispersion curvature and reduce the cut-off frequency, again WRspice
replicates the result of standard lumped element theory.

Chapter 5
Resolving WRspice Simulation and
Theory
Thus far we have shown, through simulation and experiment, that higher order tones exist in rf
SQUID embedded Josephson Travelling Wave Parametric Amplifiers (JTWPAs). Previous theories
describe an interaction of three tones, namely the pump, signal and idler. This model in the undepleted
pump case relied solely on the Difference Frequency Generation (DFG) of pump and signal(idler)
to generate(amplify) the idler(signal) tone. We have seen experimentally that higher order tones of
Sum Frequency generation (SFG) and Harmonic Generation (HG) are observed in devices. We have
then further showed that in a SPICE circuit simulator these tones cascade upwards through three wave
mixing (3WM) mechanisms to frequencies which would be difficult to measure with off the shelf
microwave circuitry.
What we would like to do is resolve the differences between the observations we have made in
previous chapters with a theory which is extended from the standard treatment to many tones. To do
this, we first want to see if we can reduce the complexity of the behaviour seen in the simulation. We
then consider the implication of orders of interaction and how this cascades throughout the device. Then
finally, we look to develop a theory which considers a logical set of frequencies defined by properties
of the device which quantitatively compare to simulation results.

Firstly, it is understood that the simulation and the CME theory will not completely agree. The
simulation includes things highlighted previously such as insertion loss, internal reflections and higher
order corrections. Also within the simulation changes in parameters are dynamic such as the phase on
the junction φ ∝arccos−1(LJ) which modifies the junction contribution to the total inductance as per
eq. (A.23). The simulations are akin to a black box theory, where we insert the inputs, crank the handle
and plot the outputs.
WRspice benefits from an extensive manual outlining the simulation procedure. However we
still confirm the results of the simulations with a theory derived from physical principles, such as
the coupled mode theory. In order to do so it is worth examining the CMEs and understanding the
assumptions made within the theory to see if we can bring the two models into a better agreement.
One assumption made was that the junction phase is set to π/2 and remains at this position such
that the inductive path through the junction is infinite, and thus the total inductance is defined by the
geometric inductance, eq. (A.23). Similarly the expansion of the rf SQUID current-phase relationship
(CPR) in eq. (1.32) relies on a small AC variation in the junction phase. In fact, from eq. (1.32) we
see that if the DC phase component φDC strays from π/2 then the odd terms in the expansion become
non-zero, while for larger AC oscillations of φ then the approximation becomes less accurate. In

Summary
In the previous sections of this chapter we have shown that by lowering both the pump and signal input
power then we can reduce the phase oscillations over the junction. The small angle approximation in
the CME theory requires that the phase shift around the DC bias point is small and for the lower power
regime we show that this assumption is now met. In reducing the power we have also seen more clearly
the preferential mixing mechanism of pump second harmonic generation. This mixing mechanism
draws energy from the pump and as a result reduces the signal amplification. We then further showed
that the cascade of harmonic generation may still be via three wave mixing, i.e still apparent in the low
power regime for small AC oscillations. This discovery forms the motivation for the key results in this
thesis and that is being able to quantitatively describe the generation of these higher order modes.

If we examine the labelled terms we get a deeper understanding of the mixing mechanisms present
within the device:
(i) This term shows that the SFG mixing of the signal and idler then generates a tone at the pump
frequency. This allows for a more general theory beyond the strong pump approximation. This
has been shown in [14], as well as previously in our three tone model and is responsible for
energy flow back and forth the pump and signal/idler tones.
(ii) This term shows that the DFG mixing of the signal and idler then generates a tone at the
difference of the two frequencies fδ. Conveniently, up until now, we have considered a signal
with a frequency fs = (3/5)fp. This means that through the 3WM condition fi = (2/5)fp and
thus fδ = (1/5)fp and that any combination of any tone considered through any further mixing
will always be a multiple of fδ. As such, there will not be a tone with a frequency lower than
fδ. To be complete; if the signal had been some irrational fraction of the pump, then an infinite
number of smaller frequency tones may be generated through cascaded difference frequency
generation.
(iii) These two terms show how a tone at fp+δ = (6/5)fp is generated through either (a) DFG
between the (pump + signal) and (idler), fp+s −fi = fδ tone or, (b) SHG of the signal fs + fs.

Note that both of these mechanisms involve no pump or pump harmonic tone and thus are not
pump mediated. By considering only pump mediated mixing (at least one tone being at the
pump or pump harmonic) we avoid an infinite number of frequencies cascading downward in
frequency, even for irrational ratios of fs/fp.
(iv) This term shows that the SFG of the pump and its own second harmonic generates a tone at the
third harmonic of the pump frequency. This is an SFG term in the 3WM case (as opposed to
THG in the 4WM regime). In the 3WM case, the coefficient of HG has a factor 1/2 which is
understood from the coefficients described by Pascal’s triangle, which is true for every order of
wave mixing.
(v) This term shows that the HG of the second harmonic of the pump generates a tone that is now at
f4p. This highlights the fact that there must be some upper bound placed on the frequencies we
consider to avoid an infinite number of frequencies cascading upward in frequency.
Here, we have highlighted the ideas behind a brute force extension to the CMEs used in section 2.2.2.
Through this method we have found that it is possible to create an infinite number of tones at frequencies
lower and higher than those previously considered.
Infinitely many, lower frequency tone generation, comes from the potential for cascaded DFG
between signal and idler tones, for signal frequencies which are some irrational ratio of the pump tone.
This is counteracted by considering only pump mediated tones, and as such, discarding any mixing
between just signal and idler such that fδ and its associated mixing products never proliferate through
the maths.
Infinitely many higher frequency tones, may be generated from the cascaded SFG and HG of tones.
This is counteracted by applying an upper limit on the maximum frequency considered, which may be
taken from simulation or theoretical models specific for each device.

Using an extension to the CMEs in comparison to the WRspice output we are able to explain and

an extension to the coupled mode equations. The brute force extension gives rise to additional tones
oscillating at various sums and differences of both the initially input tones (pump and signal) as well as
the cascade of generated tones (every other tone). This cascade of generated tones is produced in the
3WM condition, in contrast to a higher order power expansion of the Josephson relation, and thus is
still feasible in the low power limit. However, this technique is extendable to arbitrary higher order
wave mixing conditions (4WM, 5WM, ...), where only the frequency mixing conditions for each wave
mixing scenario would need to be revised. While we have purposefully moved to a lower input power
regime, which reduces the erratic behaviour of the device, we still see more complex behaviour than
initially thought. The higher order tone generation cascades through the device and interacts with each
other. In the CME-5 case there are 121 mixing mechanisms considered, compared to 3 in CME-1.

but we would expect that the fit may be improved further by including these tones. As a final message,
we show in fig. 5.15 the possible issue of not including an extended CME theory when analysing the
signal propagation and gain.

intend to develop this extended theory to include arbitrarily many tones by exploring symmetries in the

Refining this work could open many possibilities to further understanding the complex behaviour in
these devices. Firstly one is not necessarily limited to just a pump and signal input tone. If broadband
amplification is required to detect multiple signals then it stands to reason that these signals may be
input at the same time and different frequencies in the device. There is no immediately obvious reason
why this could not be considered with this type of analysis by a) giving multiple input signals at the first
step, b) then providing these input signals with a non-zero initial value in the ODE solver. Secondly

dispersion engineering is incorporated by adjusting the dispersion relationship used and subsequently
the k(ω) entered into the CMEs. We believe there is a great deal of further application for this numerical
approach to understand the complex behaviour of these devices.

6.1
Conclusion
In this thesis, I have focused on the development of a robust, practical amplifier designed for the
microwave regime, and in particular as the first stage in a chain of amplifiers targeted at quantum
information processing.
Essential requirements of such a device were; gain, G ≈20 dB over an instantaneous bandwidth
4 −8 GHz. Through measurement of partner devices and simulation of the design proposed by
Zorin [14], I discovered that broadband devices are more complex then their resonant counterparts. As
such, we refocused our efforts in to understanding this more complex behaviour outlined in chapter 4
and chapter 5 which resulted in publication [11]. These models have given us extensive insight into
previously unexpected behaviour.
In our experiments, we first show up-conversion to higher modes in devices constrained to operate
within resonant frequency conditions. The realisation of this led to the initial concern of up-conversion
in devices which have a large bandwidth of operation.
We then show the effect of cascaded harmonic generation in such a travelling wave device. We
show SHG at the three wave mixing (3WM) bias point, and THG at the four wave mixing (4WM) and
3WM bias point - THG in the 3WM demonstrates cascaded harmonic generation in JTWPAs. We also
show the Sum Frequency Generation within a travelling wave device and show that these tones can
grow larger than the idler, meaning they can no longer be neglected in the analysis of such a device.
I have extended the analytical model of three mode interactions for parametric amplification to
128
6.1. Conclusion
129
include higher order mixing processes. By solving this numerically, we can quantify the higher order
effects and their implications for the total system, including detrimental effects on signal gain in
broadband parametric amplifiers. To highlight this, we demonstrate their effects in a simulated JTWPA
and compare numerical results with simulation. It is important to consider both of these methods. The
analytic model gives functional dependencies which can help understand how the device responds to
changes in parameters. While the numerical model allows for exploration of any assumptions made in
deriving an analytic result.
In addition, we show that tools such as WRspice provide a more complete simulation than analytic
or numerical solution of CME’s can provide. We also note that WRspice has the capability to include
device parameter spreads important for devices with extensive fabrication complexity such as the
JTWPAs discussed, this is useful when evaluating experimental results. Ultimately, by understanding
the mechanisms driving these higher order tones we can then look to develop devices which counteract
the generation of unwanted tones.
One such recent development was proposed at the time of writing this thesis by Zorin [39], by
using quasi-phase matching (QPM) one can simultaneously combat up-conversion tones and promote
signal amplification. By using our extended theory we first corroborate that QPM reinforces signal gain
along the line. Also, we can demonstrate by including higher order terms that QPM actively disrupts
up-conversion to the sum frequency fp+s,i and pump second harmonic f2p; thus inhibiting the cascade
of higher order tone generation.
We then show, in WRspice, that while the signal growth does exponentially increases along the line
there are a number of considerations that must be made when comparing with theory; such as phase
stability, and pump power limitations.
Finally, we provide device parameters and optimal working points based on current technologies
with which we believe a QPM-JTWPA can achieve high-gain. We conclude that this type of engineering
which targets the core pump-signal interaction (high-gain), while broadly disrupting higher order
loss mechanisms (broad-band), provides an exciting new development for practical small-signal
amplification.

6.2
Further Work
The most pressing work is the fabrication and measurement of the rf SQUID embedded JTWPA. While
in this thesis the simulation and extension of the CME’s has shown the device may require further
engineering it is feasible that a longer (n > 2500) JTWPA may still achieve modest gain (G > 10 dB)
over a large (4 −8 GHz) bandwidth. Further optimising this design through standard engineering
techniques may yield greater gain figures and coupled with the tunability of the rf SQUID JTWPA this
may still prove vital to the community.
We expect that work on fabricating and measuring a Quasi-phase matched JTWPA to demonstrate
the proposal in [39] would yield an interesting and novel approach to increasing the performance of the
rf SQUID JTWPA. Also this approach may be used to suppress specific frequencies and as such may
be used for the specific elimination of unwanted tones. By comparing experimental results with circuit
simulations we can further verify the accuracy of our simulation and this would allow us to further
optimise designs.
We also note that the WRspice tool allows for simulation of variation of design parameters to be
easily modelled. This work has been undertaken by collaborators and the results made available via
a pre-print on the arXiv [58]. This will give more information to the community, particularly in the
design and fabrication stages of JTWPA development.
In the experiment section we noted the observation of tones spontaneously generated inside
modes of the pumped resonator. This observation is not fully understood however such instances of
spontaneous tone generation have been noted previously [59]. More recently other collaborators have
measured a similar sample and demonstrated a number of tones being generated through stimulated
wave mixing (where both pump and signal are introduced) [60]. It would be interesting to explore these
devices further to map out the regimes of both stimulated wave mixing and spontaneous emission of
tones. These may be useful for on chip tone generation in future experiments.
We also note that the generation of tones in broadband devices may be useful for certain applications.
The JTWPA has already been theoretically proposed as a squeezed state generator [61,62]. However
if a general quantum theory can be derived which maps to the extended coupled mode equations
given here then we may expect the ability to generate multiple entangled states over a large frequency
spectrum. Coupling this with the QPM engineering and tunabilty of the rf SQUID, one might propose a
6.2. Further Work

broadband, tunable, squeezed state generator for use in quantum cryptography, information processing
and quantum illumination.

B.2
WRspice Netlist
The WRspice netlist is a list of circuit elements which is then parsed to the WRspice program via
MATLAB. The .txt file is generated programatically to allow for iterative unit cells to be quickly written
to the .txt file. The junction model is defined on line 3 to have zero shunt resistance, a critical current
Ic = 5 µA, and a junction capacitance CJ = 60 fF. The pump and signal current sources are then
defined on lines 4, 5 with the phase offset, amplitude, frequency, time delay respectively. Next we
define the front end resistor with ammeters either side on lines 7, 6, 8. This is then followed by a long
list of the rf SQUID array. Following the rf SQUID netlist we include the termination resistor. This
defines the circuit however the bottom 4 lines also describe some simulation parameters. The time
step is controlled by the “.tran” line, where the time step dt = 1 ps, total time ttot = 30 ns, wait
time tdelay = 0 are defined. The time vector can also be returned from the simulation which aids in the
Fourier analysis. The uic condition tells the simulator to use any initial conditions provided which in
this example are not given. We also set the maxdata property to 8e8 as many timesteps on many
nodes can produce a lot of data.
